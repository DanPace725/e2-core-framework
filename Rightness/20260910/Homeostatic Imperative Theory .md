Neurological Imperative for Safety and Security Theory 

July 26 2023

1. **Introduction and Theory Naming**
    - Proposal of a new framework: The Neurological Imperative for Safety and Security Theory (NISST).
2. **Key Concepts and Definitions**
    - **Relationships/Connections**: Any bond or association an individual forms, including with people, objects, habits, behaviors, and ideas.
    - **Safety and Security**: The brain's neurological imperative to avoid harm (safety) and seek predictability and stability (security).
    - **Neurological Imperative**: The brain's inherent drive towards certain states or conditions.
3. **Core Propositions of NISST**
    - Relationships are a fundamental human need driven by neurological mechanisms that prioritize safety and security.
    - Insecurity can trigger primal brain states akin to hunger or thirst.
    - Relationships provide predictability and social bonding, fulfilling the brain's need for safety and stability.
    - Attachments form through the unconscious association of stimuli with safety, strengthening over time similar to neural myelination.
    - The brain may form strong attachments even to harmful relationships or addictions due to learned associations that override rational judgment.
4. **Implications and Extensions of NISST**
    - Values are derived largely from what individuals feel they need to maintain safety and security.
    - Sacrificing reciprocal human relationships (RHRs) in the pursuit of non-human connections can be less virtuous.
    - Addictions can be viewed as a biological imperative for safety that was not met by a healthier relationship.
5. **Limitations and Conclusion**
    - NISST, while potent, is not meant to explain all human behavior.
    - NISST provides a framework to examine specific behaviors and relationships.
    - The theory holds potential for explaining a significant range of behaviors in a balanced way.

- Relationships are a fundamental human need, not an optional addition to the human experience.
- The brain's hardwired drive for safety and security compels us to form attachments.
- Insecurity triggers primal survival-threat alarms like hunger or thirst.
- Relationships provide the stability, predictability and social bonds that the brain equates with safety.
- Attachments form through unconscious learned associations between stimuli and feelings of safety.
- These associations strengthen over time through repetition, like neural pathways becoming myelinated.
- Strong attachments can override rational judgements, like ingrained neural patterns.
- This explains persistence of dysfunctional bonds despite negative consequences.
- Sacrificing close relationships in pursuit of other aims may not be virtuous.
- Values derive significantly from perceptions of safety and security needs.
- Addictions can be viewed as misguided safety-seeking behaviors, not moral failures.
- This framework provides insight into core drivers of human social behaviors.
- It conceptualizes relationships through the lens of survival circuits and primal needs.

The Homeostatic Imperative Theory states that the basic driver of human behavior and connections is the search for inner stability. Inner stability means the brain keeps conditions inside the body steady and optimal. This balance gets disrupted, so the brain triggers actions to restore it.

The theory proposes that connections provide powerful ways to promote inner balance. Bonds with people, objects, activities etc. fulfill core needs the brain sees as promoting safety and stability. Strengthening these connections drives unconscious efforts towards ideal balance.

Feelings of safety basically reflect achieved inner stability. Avoiding danger protects balance. The brain forms automatic learning links between connections and signals of inner stability, which get reinforced gradually.

Other theories note how brain threat systems relate to balance needs. Human motivations put safety as a base requirement. So connections giving a sense of safety meet fundamental inner stability needs.

Through models like the neural network idea, the theory shows how relational links accumulate safety signaling weight over time to finally override danger responses. This process is captured in the safety mapping visual metaphor.

In summary, by linking diverse human social behaviors to an inborn drive for optimal inner equilibrium, this theory offers a straightforward framework for elucidating core psychological aspects through the lens of neurobiology. It proposes connections hold essential value in meeting inner stability needs that enable higher functioning.

- The Homeostatic Imperative Theory demonstrates how human relationships fulfill fundamental needs for stability and perceived safety.
- Reciprocal relationships provide neural homeostasis and security through social bonding and predictability.
- If reciprocal bonds meet core biological imperatives, strengthening them enables human flourishing.
- Therefore, from an ethical perspective, nurturing reciprocal bonds aligns with the goal of promoting human welfare.
- Relationships that provide safety are essential, not an optional extra.
- Consequently, elevating reciprocal human bonds can be considered a high moral virtue.
- Sacrificing such relationships for other pursuits mayactually be misaligned with ethical ideals.
- Immediate bonds should take priority over abstract virtues that undermine existing ties.
- We have an ethical obligation to invest in relationships that allow for mutual growth and meeting of neurological needs.
- Reciprocal human bonds enable our highest selves and moral characters to emerge.

Fundamentals of HIT:

- The core premise is that human behavior and relationships are driven by an unconscious pursuit of neural homeostasis and optimal internal stability.
- Homeostasis refers to maintaining ideal set points for bodily conditions like temperature, hydration, energy.
- Relationships help restore homeostasis by fulfilling needs for resources, bonding, predictability that map to safety.
- Subjective feelings of safety reflect achieved homeostasis. Insecurity threatens it.
- Brain forms associative learning links between relationships/stimuli and homeostasis cues, which get reinforced over time to promote survival.

Safety vs Security:

- Safety refers to immediate danger or lack thereof; security refers to abstract assurance of long-term stability.
- Safety is more foundational, arising from physiology and perception. Security requires cognitive forecasting.
- Acute unsafety restricts ability to plan for security; baseline safety enables security focus.
- Too much security focus may leave proximate safety needs unmet.
- Spectrum exists with safety as base need, security building atop it.

Introspection and Relationships:

- Introspecting one's unconscious psyche can feel threatening due to lack of familiarity.
- Conscious vs unconscious parts of brain have distinct relationships with concepts like safety.
- Infrequent introspection means examining inner workings feels unfamiliar and potentially dangerous.
- This can explain why people generally avoid plumbing depths of their own psyche.

Profiling and Spending:

- HIT could bolster psychological profiling by revealing unconscious safety motivations behind behaviors.
- Analyzing patterns in spending and financial habits through HIT lens surfaces underlying fears and insecurities.
