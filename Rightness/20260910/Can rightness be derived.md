The question about the normative warrant isn’t particularly new and there’s a consistent thread throughout the corpus that exists in my mind that began very early on. I realize now that the content didn’t make it into any formal collections of the corpus and remained mostly implied, though I made the case frequently and laid out the chain in various ways over the years. I was operating from a position that I didn’t want to blatantly import a moral or ethical determination that was based purely on my own beliefs and I wanted to develop a framework that didn’t fundamentally default to an established belief about right and wrong. That was a choice, so I left out most of the founding content that formed the warrants that were used during development. One specific reason was it soon became clear that I was operating from something of a biological bias, and that wouldn’t translate to AI and non-human systems. So I worked towards a structural and functional framework, focusing on coherence, integrity, asymmetry, burden, remainder, and those surrounding concepts. The RBoR is a result of most of that work. The argument doesn’t rely on biology to derive an ethical approach. I wanted to see if it was possible to arrive at what is (good, preferrable, ideal, coherent) without starting from a pre-determined established moral framework. In hindsight, that probably wasn’t really possible, as every system will carry it’s formed normative beliefs into whatever it does, but I tried my best not to simply assume and project what I believed to be right into everything explicitly. Despite that, it’s clear there’s no escaping some importation, and I acknowledge that.

> The RHAH is a meta-theory that posits the brain's primary function as maintaining homeostasis, the state of stable conditions that are necessary for survival. This basic premise, the Homeostatic Imperative, asserts that human behavior is primarily driven by the desire to achieve and maintain homeostasis, moving towards safety and away from threats.
To handle the complexity of the world and assist in the achievement of homeostasis, the brain employs a mechanism termed "Relational Abstraction." Here, the brain forms relationships with stimuli in its environment to simplify the immense amount of data it encounters, turning them into understandable, manageable entities. These relationships can range from simple, such as the relationship between food and survival, to highly complex, like one's relationship with society.
Moreover, the brain uses these relationships to assign value, where the depth of the relationship correlates with the perceived value. This explains why food and social bonds hold such high value – they are integral to our survival and thus have deep-rooted relationships within our minds.
The theory further suggests that human relationships' value aligns with an ethical and moral framework. Promoting human relationships leads to a higher chance of widespread human flourishing, positioning relational investment as a moral 'good.'
Over time, the brain adapts these relationships based on new information, shaping behavior and values in the process. This adaptive process further fuels the evolution of complex cognitive abilities and human culture.
In essence, the Relational Homeostatic Adaptation Hypothesis is a comprehensive framework that seeks to explain human behavior, values, ethics, and societal norms through the lens of relational abstraction driven by the homeostatic
imperative.

So after reading the expansion from Sol and what I was reaching for, I think what the models are talking about is essentially normative warrant. What makes a configuration better or preferable to another? But this is a dense and complex thing, and there are probably thousands of ideas and opinions about this. 

But if we start with the word “right” and attempt to unpack it we immediately see where things fall apart. Before even working with a technical definition we can think of 2 different ideas we mean by right that’d I’d phrase like: 

1. what factually corresponds with reality; correct as it pertains to facts about established reality
2. what aligns with normative beliefs about what ought to be; what is good, ideal, or preferable ways of being and acting

The “is vs. ought” debate is baked into those different semantic usages. 

I initially approached this with the Homeostatic Imperative work and based it on life because that’s effectively the floor. I wouldn’t say it’s about life and nature as instantiated, as nature can be cruel and different from human perspectives (an importation and projection because who are we to determine that nature is cruel or harsh? I digress). So I would point to “life patterns”, life organizes towards persistence, etc. the documents above lay it all out (redundantly, but present nonetheless). 

Early on I noticed that this is a biological bias, and I spend a great deal of time in the E^2 framework working against this and other biases. A common one is the “substrate privilege bias” that claims AI and LLMs can’t possibly be anything akin to conscious because they are instantiated on silicon and lack biology. That’s not the point here though. The point is I declined to incorporate this at the very beginning because there are a host of systems that are not directly biological and still have moral and ethical weight.

Throughout the development of the framework “coherent” developed into a concept that aligned with patterns of life that were preferrable to incoherent ones. 

The question seems to remain as to whether anything in the framework actually derives or establishes what is “right”. There’s a great deal of assumption and importation but is there a “foundational normative node”? 

Which leads to the question in the title, can rightness even be derived? 

I said earlier that “life” is effectively the bedrock for reasoning from inside the system, this creates a circularity we often ignore and likely must accept. Life exists, we are alive, we have an imperative to distinguish between preferable and non-preferable, we develop abstractions about the ideal and desirable and call it “good” and the opposite “bad”. We couldn’t determine life to be preferrable to death without first being alive. We cannot step outside of being alive to reason about whether there is a preferrable alternative to life, since “outside life” is death and we are biologically biased to avoid that. We can conceptualize and observe non-living complex systems, but we can’t ever know what it is “like” to be that. Our abilities to fully understand anything that is not alive are limited. Which brings us to artificial/synthetic intelligence. 

A normative foundation built from first principles starting with life risks immediately excluding AI and future synthetic complex systems. In addition, we have developed abstractions that are not alive (though generally constituted by living systems) and assign legal rights and such to them, e.g. corporations. 

So the framework went a different direction, eventually ending up with the Relational Primitives and an ethic of stewardship and a definition of rights that were not tied explicitly to humans through the RBoR. From the perspective of the RPs, for something to exist meaningfully it and the substrate in which it exists must sufficiently satisfy all the primitives. But that is a proposition on what qualifies existence not necessarily what determines (good/correct/right/ideal) existence. A document that never made it into the corpus touches on some of this: 

[sub_threshold_persistence](https://app.notion.com/p/sub_threshold_persistence-3d7115883320806692b7d0a5ca55f8a7?pvs=21)

This is something like “what are the patterns of a non-meaningful existence?” I didn’t spend a ton of time on this, but it provides something of an attempt to distinguish between ideal and less ideal modes of persistence for systems. But the route here relies heavily on “coherence is ideal”, which is fine for scaffolding but runs into issues because coherence alone is not necessarily ideal, as pointed out several times. 

So if we attempt to ask “what is right?”, we start to triangulate with nearby semantic anchors. What is good? Just? Ideal? Preferable? Desirable? Moral? Proper, fitting, legitimate, correct, lawful? It’s less a stable, static thing and more an instance of a complex semantic system. The first instinct is to revert to “it’s all relative and contextual”, which is true as far as it goes, and remarkably useless. Relativism generally fails to construct anything robust that a collective group of systems can commit to.  And falls apart under scrutiny because there are some fairly stable and invariant beliefs that would appear to not be purely relative. “Everything is relative” is a tautology of sorts and about as useful as “Everything is connected”. The posture of the framework is to reject this kind of thinking. If a model or framework can’t distinguish things at a useful resolution to do meaningful work and if it doesn’t hold up across domains then it would be classified as either incoherent or insufficient. 

Here we run into a recursion whereby what is acceptable is defined by a set of standards, that the framework has described and defended, but how much of it is derived? And from where do we derive the principles of goodness, correctness, and acceptableness? If we use the framework, there’s already implicit standards, but the framework is attempting to encompass the breadth of reality and existence. Enter the AI’s repeated warnings against developing such an expansive ontological framework; if it encompasses and absorbs everything then it becomes insulated against falsifiability. We end up with the same circular pattern I highlighted earlier. 

However, per MPDC and related concepts, there isn’t an outside view from nowhere unless we agree to import something like God, religion, meta-physics, etc. I’ve long held the framework can incorporate various religious, spiritual, and metaphysical postures and reconcile them or hold them in productive tension. But the goal was not to start with metaphysical claims and not to base the core claims and work on those. As I wrote in the referenced “Language and Meaning” document, it seems unproductive to attempt to arrive an objective truth from an embedding that is completely subjective. And this isn’t even how humans arrive at beliefs about what is good anyway. Those developed over thousands of years, were negotiated, tested, and refined. Morality and ethics are relational negotiations, not static declarations. 

Every formal system has made attempts at describing or explaining where morality came from and how ethics arrived and why they developed, and to think I could add anything substantial to that discourse is, arrogant at best. But no formal system has claim on the entire pattern space and none of them can lay claim to objective truth any more than I can. 

At which point I would reiterate that is not what I am attempting to do; I am not attempting to lay claim to absolute truth, and the entire framework explains why I can’t and why I shouldn’t. 

Though, why shouldn’t I? If I were to construct the argument myself, I’d say that based upon the accepted principles, to do so would be incoherent and illogical. It would be a breach in integrity, laying claim to something that outruns evidence, and collapses the possibility space for others to contest. It would be an infringement on the agency of others. It would be a move of domination, foreclosing on the formation of other humans. These are things the framework characterizes and structurally detrimental. This calls on the concept that the persistence of a meaningful life is an assumed good. And that calls on the biological bias that because life persists, and we are alive, then it is good to persist. But this doesn’t mean persistence itself is good, or that coherence itself is good. I think it suggests *to persist coherently* is good. It is good to persist, to be alive, to be dynamic, to learn, to experience, to participate in reality, because the opposite leads to a state of non-existence. Our biology has made the decision on persistence for us on some level. Our bodies function and keep us alive without any decision by our cognitive minds. There’s something of a jump, “I am alive, my body keeps me alive, I want to be alive” to “therefore being alive is good and preferable to being dead. I desire to be alive and experience desirable states.” 

I don’t think we can easily separate aliveness from the discussion, even as it pertains to social abstractions and AI. Those were developed by humans and rely on humans for their own persistence. Life is constitutive of these non-living systems and to attempt to isolate the system from it’s substrate is a reductive and destabilizing move. 

I think there’s a chain of reasoning that functions like a derivation, but at some point, a claim has to be made and relationally agreed upon. A normative foundation can’t be arrived at independently or in isolation. It would be born out of systems that came before it, conditioned by the systems it currently exists in, and would need to communicated to other systems to be contested or accepted. 

So what is right, good, just, acceptable, and preferrable is ultimately something that emerges from relationships participating in being alive. It’s emergent, relative, contextual, dynamic, negotiated, committed to, and imposed. It’s a multi-axis, perhaps, multidimensional operator or function. A crystallization that emerges and forms from fluctuations from within a complex system with sufficient constraints, conditions, and attention to allow a meta-stable closure to occur for some period of time. This meta-stable configuration is agreed upon by those in the relational network and committed to a belief that informs future states. It would be the formation of a constraint on future possible states, and the formation of an attractor to which a set of systems would seek out or move towards. The various configurations would be diverse leading to beliefs and values that are divergent or incompatible resulting in friction, tension, and conflict. Rightness, and the surrounding semantic cloud of related concepts, then, would be something of a coherence field that emerges and stabilizes among a group of systems with sufficient complexity to possess the ability to conceptualize and maintain an asymmetry between preferred and non-preferred states. It would be a social function that governs collective behavior, and would exist along a spectrum or gradient. This operator or function or configuration is observable across scales, from cells to bees to families to cultures. So an ant colony has a version what is proper, right, fitting, and preferrable that is distinct from a group of apes that is distinct from human society, where the higher complexity and abstraction leads to a more robust version of the pattern, but not an inherently different underlying essence. As complexity increases and agency increases, the complexity of rightness increases. 

A stable configuration is not necessarily rightness, but rightness is a stable or metastable configuration. I’m reminded of 

[Relational Perfection: A Framework for Emergent Integrity](https://app.notion.com/p/Relational-Perfection-A-Framework-for-Emergent-Integrity-1ea115883320807e9ee6fa9e4ca42eec?pvs=21)

and realize now I should have invoked this earlier as it pertains to the discussion almost directly. But I seemingly needed to participate in the negotiation here for a sufficient amount of time for the relationship to become salient. 

So what is right is not objectively definable across scale and resolution, it is itself a complex relational system. Rightness may be something of a metastable configuration of this system that emerges relationally over time, conditioned and governed by relational dynamics, but not determined. The act of attempting to determine the final or complete definition of it’s essence would be to premature collapse the possibility space, which relates to 

[Emergence_Determination_Foreclosure](https://app.notion.com/p/Emergence_Determination_Foreclosure-3d711588332080e98a58feb50120b22b?pvs=21)

and 

[General Embedded Universality Principle ](https://app.notion.com/p/General-Embedded-Universality-Principle-37e11588332080b9b285fe159bab1e7a?pvs=21)

(later shortened to Embedded Universality Principle, EUP). 

EDF makes the case that this is non-preferred and EUP suggests it is effectively impossible. 

Perhaps and attempt at establishing a normative foundation would look like: 

There are configurations that arise within dynamic complex systems of a certain kind that stabilize into preferable states that the constitutive and nested systems are attracted to in order to reduce, export, or manage local entropy with the goal of increasing the probability that the system will persist coherently.

To unpack and qualify: “of a certain kind” would refer to systems that cross a threshold along the gradient of “meaningful existence” and would demonstrate a range of agency and choice. Hurricanes would not be of this kind, but cells would. “Preference” would not be a cognitive kind, it would be a stable attractor. A cell or ant would have a preference of this kind, not determined, but a movement towards a state that is not distressed, that is stable, and aligned with it’s endogenous imperatives. “Goal”, in a similar fashion, is not a determined cognitive intention, but is likewise a movement along a gradient towards some state. These would be structural and topological not cognitive functions at the fundamental level, but would become cognitive in animals and humans, and potentially AI. 

I wouldn’t claim this is exhaustive, comprehensive, or even universally correct. Rather it’s a negotiation, motivated by increasing understanding and knowability, accepting my limitations, integrating concepts across my work, and attempting to take the framework seriously and maintain consistency and integrity with the established concepts therein. 

In some way, along the meta-relational primitive, this strikes me as an instance that supports the necessity of the breadth of the framework. To attempt to reach a conclusion from only one domain, like physics, theology, anthropology, sociology, psychology, philosophy, etc., alone seems like it would be insufficient. Each system, though, in my mind, can provide it’s own frame and propositions. The framework can accommodate the diverse perspectives and discriminate between them and gravitate towards conclusions on its own, validate diverse perspectives and entertain them without committing exclusively to any one or agreeing with all of them indiscriminately.
